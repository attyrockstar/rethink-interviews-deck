From hiring, and from being hired

Three lessons worth internalising

  1. Credibility built before you walk through the door is priceless
    The interviewer who spent thirty minutes on your writing is not interviewing you. They are confirming a decision they already made.
  2. PM roles usually require prior PM experience
    The catch-22. You cannot get the job without the experience, and you cannot get the experience without the job. Part 02 is how you break it.
  3. Job hunting makes you sharper
    Applying, researching and interviewing is itself a product education. You come out of the process a better thinker than you went in.
Rethink Systems.
Lesson one, drawn out

What is in front of them the night before

The second conversation is not an evaluation. It is a confirmation. Credibility built before you walk through the door is priceless, and it is built over months, not in the week the opening appears.

Rethink Systems.
Lesson three, drawn out

The search is not dead time. It is the education.

Every company you research is a free product teardown. Every interview is a live case study with a domain expert. Candidates who treat the search as overhead learn nothing from it. Candidates who treat it as practice arrive at the offer already better than they were.

Rethink Systems.
Take a step back

Every interview question is a proxy for one of five attributes

01
Has product sense
Usually makes the right call, at the macro and the micro level
02
Is smart
Structures an unfamiliar problem without being handed a framework
03
Gets things done
Ships. Has a track record of things that actually reached users
04
Is a culture fit
Works the way this specific team works, not the way PMs work in general
05
Has technical skills
Domain expertise plus a real understanding of the technology

Marty Cagan's version: the product manager is responsible for discovering a product that is useful, usable and feasible. The five attributes are what that takes.

Rethink Systems.
Attribute one, defined
Product sense is the ability to usually make the right product decision.
Both macro and micro. Note the word usually. Nobody is scoring you on being right every time.
Rethink Systems.
Attribute one, tested in the room

“Why does Zomato show the delivery time before you have picked a dish?”

Obvious
“So the user knows when the food will arrive.”
True, and every candidate says it. It restates the feature.
Interesting
“It sets the expectation before the commitment, so fewer people cancel after ordering.”
Now you are reasoning about behaviour, not the screen.
Profound
“The time promise is the product. Restaurants are inventory. Whoever owns the promise owns the customer.”
This is the answer the interviewer repeats to the panel. One of these per interview is enough.

Illustrative answers, not Zomato's stated strategy. The point is the ladder, not the company.

Rethink Systems.
Attribute five, defined

Technical skills = domain expertise + understanding the technology

What is being tested
Can you explain the basics of the technologies your product depends on?
Not write the code. Explain the system: what calls what, what breaks, what it costs, why a change is a week and not a day.
Why it carries weight
Engineering usually has a large say in the hiring decision
In most PM loops there is an engineering interviewer with real veto power. They are not checking your syntax. They are checking whether working with you will be expensive.
Rethink Systems.
The answer that ends the loop
“I am a business person. Next question, please.”
As a PM, technology is your business. This answer reads as an unwillingness to learn, and it travels back to the panel.

Getting real PM experience
before you have the title
How to break the catch-22 without spending two years and thirty lakhs on it.
Before anything else: be sure you will actually enjoy the job

Responsibility without authority is the job. Talk to at least eight product managers about the good and, more usefully, the bad. If the bad still sounds like a fair trade, continue.

Rethink Systems.
Those eight conversations are not just research

Referrals are 6% of applications and 37% of hires

A referred candidate is roughly six times as likely to be hired as one who applied cold. The eight PMs you talk to about the bad parts of the job are also the eight people who can put your name in front of a hiring manager. Global hiring data; the Indian ratio is not published, and nobody who has hired here believes it is lower.

Wrong answer: business school

Rethink Systems.
Spend twenty hours with the best free career-research tool on the planet

LinkedIn is not for posting. It is for reading paths.

  1. Find thirty PMs who started where you are now
    Same college tier, same first function, same city. Filter by title, then read backwards. The pattern in thirty profiles is worth more than any career-advice thread.
  2. Write down the move that got each of them the title
    Internal transfer. Founder's office. APM programme. A startup nobody had heard of. Count them. That distribution is your realistic set of doors.
  3. Message the eight whose path is closest to yours
    Not “can I pick your brain”. One specific question about the one move you are considering. Most will answer. Some will become your referrals.
Rethink Systems.
The catch-22 is real. The gate is not what you think it is.

71 of every 100 PMs in India have no software-engineering degree

Belong analysed the education of 15,000 software PMs in India. Only 29% held a software-engineering degree. Only 6% had the engineering-plus-MBA combination everyone assumes is the ticket.

The people who got in came from business, operations, design, analytics and sales. What they had in common was not the degree. It was proof they had already done parts of the job.

Belong.co research on 15,000 Indian PM profiles. Directional; the study predates the AI-era hiring shift.

Rethink Systems.
Where should you go to become a PM?

Right answer: your current company

Get hands-on PM experience where you already have context, credibility and access. You are after the skills and the perspective that make people take you seriously when you apply. The observation that makes this work: PMs are busy, and can always use help from a capable colleague.

Rethink Systems.
Four moves, starting Monday

How to do it from where you sit

  1. Approach your PM and offer to help
    Be specific: what you will do, for how long, and what the goal is. “I will run the weekly user-complaint triage for the next eight weeks and bring you the top three patterns.” A vague offer is easy to decline.
  2. Shadow PMs in their core team meetings
    You are learning how decisions get made and how disagreement gets resolved. That is the part no course teaches.
  3. Become the biggest source of ideas for the product you already work on
    Then implement them. Ideas alone make you a commentator. Shipped ideas make you hard to ignore.
  4. Take a good PM class
    Especially useful for picking up the vocabulary you will be expected to speak fluently in interviews.
Rethink Systems.
The objection everyone has
“This is too much work. Is there an easier way?”
Being resourceful under constraint is the job. Treat this as the first test of your suitability. Give it six months, then put the experience at the top of your search. More doors will open for you than any degree opens.
Rethink Systems.
The credibility play

Write about the practice of product management

Compared with other fields there is remarkably little written about how the job is actually done. That gap is your opening. “I have nothing to write about” is almost never true:

How to manage a launch
How to run an effective exec review
Five mistakes I made and what I learned
An essential reading list for PMs
How to work with sales, engineering, design
A product you love, and how you would improve it
Rethink Systems.
Nobody:
Every aspiring PM's LinkedIn headline:
“Passionate about building 0 to 1 products at the intersection of technology, business and design.”
What credibility actually looks like:
Six posts, over six months, on how the job is actually done. Read by the person interviewing you, the night before.
Rethink Systems.
03
The pre-interview
assignment
The highest-leverage thing most candidates never do.
Why bother

A resume is your highlights. An assignment is your thinking.

What a resume can show
Your most featured highlights. Where you worked, what shipped, which numbers moved. Real, but curated, and it says very little about the acumen you hold.
What an assignment shows
How you break down a problem you were not briefed on. Two signals land at once: you cared enough to do unpaid work, and the interviewer can see you think.

More than background, a good product company is hiring a good thinker.

Rethink Systems.
The work does three jobs at once

You are preparing while you are impressing

Company
You learn what they actually do
Not the careers page version. What the product is, who uses it, where the money comes from.
Product
You learn where they are heading
Recent releases tell you the bets. Swiggy folding Super into Swiggy One, across food, Instamart and Dineout, tells you more about the roadmap than any job description will.
Role
You learn what they are hiring for
Read the LinkedIn profiles of their current PMs. If most came from design, design is the emphasis. Prepare accordingly.
Rethink Systems.
A worked example

Groww has a PM opening. Here is the hour you spend.

  1. Open the app and use it properly
    Groww is India's largest investment platform by NSE active clients: 12.58 million, a 26% share, listed since November 2025. Go through the flow the way a first-time investor would, not the way an analyst would.
  2. Understand how investing actually happens there
    Onboarding, KYC, discovery, the decision moment, the follow-through after you have put money in.
  3. Map it against real people you know
    Your own investing pattern, and the pattern of the people around you. The gaps show up in the distance between the two.
  4. Pick one gap and write the assignment
    Trust, customer service and expert advice are three places worth digging. A platform that went from 5 million to 12.5 million clients in two years has onboarded a lot of first-timers. One problem statement, not four.

Groww RHP, June 2025 figures.

Rethink Systems.
What the assignment looks like when it is done

One page. Six boxes. Nothing else.

1 · Problem
First-time Groww users who buy one fund and never return
One sentence. Name the user and the behaviour, not the feature.
2 · Evidence
What you saw, what you counted, who you asked
Five conversations and your own usage beat a survey you did not run.
3 · Why now
12.5M clients, most of them new to markets
Tie it to a real number from their filings or releases.
4 · Proposal
One intervention, sketched
A wireframe or a flow. Not three options with pros and cons.
5 · Metric
Second-purchase rate within 30 days
One number that moves if you are right, and a guess at how much.
6 · Risks
What breaks, what you would need to learn
Naming your own blind spots is the strongest signal on the page.

Send it before the first round, with the resume. If it is good, the interview starts on your page, not theirs.

Rethink Systems.
04
The resume
Five sections. Facts do the work, not adjectives.
The one rule underneath all five sections
Impress with facts, not with big words that make you look important.
Because they do not. PMs are judged on actionable points, and the worst thing you can hand a hiring manager is a resume full of non-actionable jargon. The summary drives a go or no-go decision in under a minute: keep it short and real.
Rethink Systems.
Section 01

Objective

Weak
A visionary product management executive with vast strategic experience driving operational efficiencies at Fortune 100 enterprises with C-level visibility.

Every word is doing PR. Nothing is verifiable, so nothing is memorable.

Strong
Five years building enterprise monitoring products for IBM, HP and Applied Materials. Those products generated more than 200 million in revenue.

Same length. Every claim checkable.

Rethink Systems.
Section 01, if you have no PM experience yet

The same rule still applies

Final-year student interested in product management and building user-centric products. Led the college fest committee across three online events with 500+ participants, and drove 20 lakhs in revenue.

You have no PM title. You do have scope, constraint and a number. That is the whole recipe. Enthusiasm is not a qualification, but what you did with your enthusiasm is.

Rethink Systems.
Section 02

Experience

The trap
Writing everything about the product and everything you personally did on it.

Three bullets maximum per role. Last three or four roles only. Always speak to user impact, never to the intermediate changes that produced it.

What each bullet answers
  • What was the product
  • Why was it special, meaning what was the impact
  • What key challenge did you overcome
Led the live class platform at BYJU'S. Drove personalised 1:25 classes to over 100k students a day. Built the strategy for student-to-student and student-to-tutor relationships at scale.
Rethink Systems.
Section 02, the rewrite most of this room needs

You are not a PM yet. Your bullets can still read like one.

Before: a city-ops lead at a quick-commerce company
Managed dark-store operations for Bengaluru. Coordinated with multiple cross-functional stakeholders. Handled customer escalations and ensured SLA adherence. Drove process improvements.

Four sentences, zero numbers, and every verb could describe anyone in the building.

After: same job, written for a PM screen
Ran 14 dark stores in Bengaluru, about 40,000 orders a week. Cut order-to-dispatch time from 9 to 6 minutes by redesigning the picking route with the store leads. Escalations per 1,000 orders fell by a third in two months.

Product, impact, challenge. A hiring manager can now ask “how did you redesign the route?” and you have an hour of material.

Illustrative profile. The numbers are the shape, not a real person.

Achievements

This section has one job: plant hooks that make the interviewer curious enough to ask. A good hook becomes a conversation you control, in an hour where most things are being asked of you.

With experience
Awarded a 3,000 dollar bounty by Uber for finding bugs in their app.
Without experience
Raised 50 lakhs of funding in college for an event. Or: among the first five teams to build [thing] on campus.

Research well before you apply

Three kinds, in this order

01 Industry

Rethink Systems.
Turning the inference into preparation

Each background produces a predictable question type

Design-leaning interviewer
“How would you design a bathroom for someone who is blind?”
Practise reasoning from the user's constraint outward, not from the feature inward.
Business-leaning interviewer
“Should Udemy move into live learning?”
Practise taking a position, then defending it against the strongest counter-argument.
Analytics-leaning interviewer
“How many Teslas are sold in New York in a day?”
Practise estimation out loud. The number is irrelevant. The structure is the answer.
Rethink Systems.
What most PM and APM loops contain

Six stages, rarely in a different order

  1. Tell me about yourself
    Sell the story. This is the only question you can fully prepare, and most people waste it.
  2. What is product management, and why are you a fit for it?
  3. How would you improve our product, or a product of your choice?
  4. An assignment based on the business
  5. A technical round, sometimes
  6. Compensation
Rethink Systems.
Stage one, done properly
The interviewer wants to interview you if they think they will take something away from the hour.
Your story is the thing that makes that likely. Build it deliberately.
Rethink Systems.
“Tell me about yourself” has a shape

Ninety seconds. Four beats. Then stop talking.

The most common failure is a four-minute chronological walk from school onwards. The interviewer stops listening at ninety seconds. Give them the problem, the place, the path and the reason for the switch, then hand the conversation back.

Rethink Systems.
A good story answers three questions

Problem, place, path

  1. Why did this problem interest you?
    Say it at the scale you actually think at. Solving payments for 300 million students. Solving learning for a 15 billion dollar economy. Then give it a relatable handle: “Stripe for India”.
  2. Why does the place you worked excite you?
    Describe the growth or the business model, not the brand. “Shopify for India, at this stage of growth, with this model.”
  3. Why does your path make sense, and why is this switch next?
    If you have moved quickly between jobs, own it with a clear through-line. A short Loom explaining your story works as a visual resume and almost nobody sends one.

On product sense questions

There is a line, and most answers never cross it

You do not need to be profound for the whole hour. You need it once. Pick the product you know most deeply and find the one observation about it that the interviewer has not heard before.

Rethink Systems.
On product exercises

Three shapes it takes, and the only tip that matters

Shape one
Write a mini-PRD
A page: user, problem, proposal, metric, risks. The one-pager from Part 03 is exactly this.
Shape two
Draw rough wireframes
Boxes and arrows on paper are fine. They are testing whether you can make a decision visible, not whether you own Figma.
Shape three
Present a proposal on X
Ten minutes, out loud, with a recommendation. The hardest of the three because you cannot hide behind the document.
“It usually takes me more than three weeks to prepare a good impromptu speech.”Mark Twain. Practice is the whole tip.
Rethink Systems.
The structure that works for any product exercise

Five moves, in this order, every time

  1. Clarify the objective before you answer
    “When you say improve, is that revenue, retention or reach?” Thirty seconds that saves the other nine minutes.
  2. Cover the top issues first, then go into detail
    Name the three things that matter, pick one, go deep. Never depth on the first thing you thought of.
  3. Show the options you considered
    Two or three, briefly. The interviewer is scoring your judgement, and judgement is visible only against alternatives.
  4. Point out the drawbacks of your own choice
    Before they do. It is the single strongest signal of product sense in the room.
  5. It is fine to say “I don't know”
    Followed by how you would find out. Bluffing is the one thing that reliably ends a loop.
Rethink Systems.
Practice, corollary

If you absolutely love a company, do not interview there first

  • You get noticeably better after your first two or three interviews anywhere.The nerves, the pacing, the “tell me about yourself” all settle. Spend that learning curve somewhere you can afford to lose.
  • Schedule the dream company after that.Two or three loops elsewhere first, then the one that matters.
  • Know their products really well, and know that you still lack context.Come with ways you would improve them. Say out loud that you are missing what they know internally. Both halves matter.

The choice
Congratulations, you have some offers. Now choose carefully, because some PM jobs usually suck.
Four kinds of PM job that usually suck

Spot them in the interview, before you are in them

01
The PM without any engineers
You own a strategy, and someone else owns the roadmap. Every feature is a negotiation with a peer who does not report to you.
02
The company where engineering is not a core strength
Engineering becomes a service organisation. Good engineers leave, and the ones who stay are not engaged in the product.
03
The company that does not understand product management
You were hired to carry documents between departments that do not talk. Your title says PM. Your job is courier.
04
The PM who is not in charge of an actual product
Corollary: the PM who reports into marketing. If nothing ships because of you, you are not doing the job you were hired for.

One question to ask every interviewer: “Walk me through the last thing a PM here shipped, and who decided it.” The answer tells you which of these four you are looking at.

The one rule for choosing
Join a company that gets product management, builds good products, and has smart engineers.
